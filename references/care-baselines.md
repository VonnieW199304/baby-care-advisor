# Care Baselines

Use these baselines when the user asks for general caregiving, play, early-learning, routine, or caregiver-plan advice without requesting a named method. They are a decision floor, not a script to print in every answer.

## 1. Safety And Health Override

- Immediate health, injury, feeding, medication, sleep-safety, or product hazards override method fidelity and convenience.
- A parenting philosophy cannot waive current local medical advice, an approved product instruction, or a relevant safety rule.
- When risk is uncertain, choose a reversible interim action and identify the observation or source that would change it.

## 2. Responsive Caregiving

- Notice, interpret, and respond to the baby's signals in a timely and appropriate way.
- Responsiveness does not require giving the same response every time or removing every frustration. The response can be feeding, comfort, a pause, environmental adjustment, help with regulation, or space to continue trying.
- Do not infer manipulation or moral intent from crying, clinging, refusal, repeated bids for help, or changing needs.

This baseline is consistent with [WHO nurturing care](https://www.who.int/teams/maternal-newborn-child-adolescent-health-and-ageing/child-health/nurturing-care) and [UNICEF Care for Child Development](https://www.unicef.org/documents/care-child-development).

## 3. Developmental Fit And Individual Variation

- Match activities, expectations, language, texture, equipment, and help to current ability rather than age averages alone.
- Use corrected age when appropriate and known, but do not invent it.
- Support emerging ability without forcing a milestone, holding a posture the child cannot sustain, or turning normal variation into a training deficit.
- Loss of acquired skills, persistent functional difficulty, or several related concerns requires a different route from ordinary variation.

## 4. Safe Environment, Agency, And Exploration

- Change the environment before repeatedly correcting developmentally normal exploration.
- Offer a small number of reachable, age-appropriate materials and enough uninterrupted time to observe what the child initiates.
- Help only as much as needed for safety, regulation, access, or the next achievable step.
- Independence is not withdrawal of care. Agency and reliable caregiver availability can coexist.

## 5. Learning Through Ordinary Interaction

- Conversation, imitation, shared attention, singing, books, movement, object exploration, and participation in care can support learning without a formal lesson.
- Follow the child's attention often enough for interaction to remain reciprocal; introduce variation without demanding performance.
- Do not equate more stimulation, more toys, earlier teaching, or a branded course with faster or better development.

## 6. Predictability Without Rigidity

- Predictable sequences, cues, and repeated routines can reduce coordination cost and help the family observe patterns.
- A routine is a hypothesis about what usually works, not a reason to ignore hunger, illness, pain, fatigue, developmental change, or an unusual day.
- Prefer anchors and ranges over minute-level schedules unless a clinician has given a specific plan.
- Evaluate a routine by feeding adequacy, sleep opportunity, distress, development, caregiver capacity, and whether it can adapt.

## 7. Caregiver Sustainability

- A plan that requires constant vigilance, excessive tracking, or one caregiver doing everything is unlikely to remain effective.
- Include caregiver sleep, recovery, mental load, support, values, and household constraints when they materially affect the plan.
- Sustainable care does not mean maximizing convenience at the child's expense; it means finding a safe arrangement the family can consistently carry out.

## 8. Small Experiments And Review

- Change one material factor when practical, define what improvement would look like, and observe long enough to see a pattern.
- Stop or revise when distress, feeding, sleep, safety, or family functioning worsens.
- Do not turn a successful one-family routine into a universal developmental claim.

## Conflict Order

When recommendations conflict, resolve them in this order:

1. Immediate safety and medical needs.
2. Adequate feeding, sleep opportunity, comfort, and basic care.
3. The child's current signals, ability, and functional needs.
4. Family constraints and caregiver sustainability.
5. Method fidelity, aesthetics, convenience, and ideology.

The method is the first thing to adapt, not the child or the safety boundary.
